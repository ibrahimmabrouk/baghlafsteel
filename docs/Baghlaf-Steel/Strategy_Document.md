think **this is the point where we should stop thinking about tools and start thinking about the business itself**.

The most important next step is not Figma, not Claude Code, and not even the sitemap.

It is to create a **single source of truth for Baghlaf Steel** that answers:

> **Who is Baghlaf Steel, what exactly does it do, why should an international buyer trust it, and what can we safely claim on the website?**

Everything else—Figma, content, SEO, AI coding, animations, even the choice of components—should come after that.

I'd build the project around **6 interconnected layers**:

```
                    BAGHLAF STEEL

                          ▼
```

The key is that **technology is layer 6, not layer 1**.

# 1. Identity — Who is Baghlaf Steel?

This needs to be extremely precise because of the relationship with Baghlaf Holding.

We need to establish internally:

### Company identity

- Exact legal company name
- Commercial registration name
- Brand name
- Official English spelling
- Official Arabic spelling
- Country
- Headquarters
- Year established
- Ownership/management wording that the company approves
- Exact relationship, if any, with other Baghlaf entities

### Very important

We should **not write a public statement about the separation from Baghlaf Holding based solely on the information you've received**.

Instead, we should ask management to give us the exact approved wording.

For example, perhaps the approved language is:

> "Baghlaf Steel operates as an independent company within the Baghlaf family."

Or something entirely different.

That distinction matters because the website will become a public corporate document.

---

# 2. Positioning — What does Baghlaf actually sell?

This is where our previous understanding changes.

Baghlaf is not saying:

> "We manufacture steel."

It is saying:

> **"We source and supply steel."**

And that gives us a potentially powerful positioning:

### Baghlaf Steel

**Saudi Steel Trading & Supply**

Then the business proposition becomes:

> International buyers provide their steel requirements, and Baghlaf coordinates sourcing and supply through qualified manufacturers in Saudi Arabia.

That is the foundation.

---

# 3. Trust — This should become the central theme

This is where I think the project becomes much more interesting than a normal corporate website.

We originally thought the objective was:

> "Make a professional steel company website."

I now think the actual objective is:

> **"Create a verifiable digital identity that gives international buyers confidence that they are dealing directly with the real Baghlaf Steel."**

That means the website needs to establish **three different kinds of trust**.

### A. Corporate trust

Who are you?

```
Company
Registration
Office
Management
Contact information
Official domain
Official email
```

### B. Commercial trust

Can you actually fulfill the requirement?

```
Products
Specifications
Supply network
Order process
Commercial process
Documentation
Logistics
```

### C. Digital trust

Is this actually the official company?

```
Official website
Official email domain
Official phone
LinkedIn
Verification page
Authorized contacts
Anti-impersonation notice
```

This third layer is unusual—and highly relevant to Baghlaf.

---

# 4. We should define the buyer before we define the pages

I don't want the website to be designed around generic terms like:

> "Our customers are businesses."

Let's define the actual visitor.

For example:

### Buyer A — International Procurement Manager

Arrives with:

- Product requirement
- Quantity
- Grade
- Standard
- Delivery destination
- Timeline

He wants to know:

> "Can these people source what I need?"

### Buyer B — Steel Trader

Wants:

> "Can Baghlaf supply large quantities reliably and commercially?"

### Buyer C — First-time buyer

His first question may actually be:

> **"Is this company legitimate?"**

That third persona is especially important because of your market problem.

---

# 5. The website should therefore answer questions in the buyer's order

Not in the company's preferred order.

A buyer might mentally go:

```
Who are you?
      ↓
Are you legitimate?
      ↓
What do you supply?
      ↓
Where does the steel come from?
      ↓
Can you fulfill my quantity/specification?
      ↓
How does the process work?
      ↓
What documents / commercial process do you use?
      ↓
How do I contact the real company?
```

That should directly influence the website architecture.

---

# 6. I would build the site around "proof", not just information

This is one of the biggest strategic decisions I'd make.

A normal corporate site says:

> About us  
> Our products  
> Our services  
> Contact us

A Baghlaf site should feel more like:

> **Who we are**  
> **What we supply**  
> **How we source**  
> **How we work**  
> **Why buyers trust us**  
> **How to verify us**  
> **Submit your requirement**

That is a much stronger B2B structure.

# 8. "Supply Network" could become a major differentiator

This is something I would explore carefully.

Instead of pretending Baghlaf has a factory, we explain the network model:

But before publishing the names/logos of Hadeed, Al-Rajhi Steel, Al-Ittefaq, Al-Watania, etc., we need management to confirm **exactly what relationship can legally and commercially be stated**.

That becomes part of our strategy document.

# 9. We also need a "Claims Matrix"

This is something I strongly recommend for this project.

Because AI will eventually write lots of copy, we need to prevent it from inventing corporate claims.

Create a table like:

|Claim|Status|Evidence/Source|Publicly Allowed?|
|---|---|---|---|
|Saudi-based company|Confirmed|Company docs|Yes|
|Steel trading company|Confirmed|Management|Yes|
|Steel manufacturer|False|—|**Never use**|
|Sources from Hadeed|To verify|Management|Pending|
|Official partner of Hadeed|To verify|Contract|Pending|
|Minimum order 100,000 MT|Management statement|Contract/management|Pending|
|International supplier|Confirmed|Management|Yes|
|Official domain = baghlaf.com.sa|Pending registration|Registrar|Pending|

This protects us from one of the biggest dangers of AI-generated corporate websites:

**beautifully written false claims.**

# 10. The same document should define "forbidden language"

For example:

```
Never say:
"We manufacture..."
"Our factories..."
"Our production capacity..."
"Our steel mill..."

Unless explicitly approved and factually supported.
```

And also:

```
Avoid:
"Leading"
"World-class"
"Number one"
"Unmatched"
"Guaranteed"
"Official partner"
"Authorized distributor"

unless supported by evidence.
```

The site should sound **credible**, not inflated.

For an international buyer, that distinction matters.
